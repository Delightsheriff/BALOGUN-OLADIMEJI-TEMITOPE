CHAPTER ONEINTRODUCTION
1.1 Background to the Study
According to a recent survival analysis of student dropouts in a Nigerian university system, a startling 85.05% of students who started with a third-class CGPA eventually dropped out before finishing their programme, and 41.42% of male students and 38.94% of female students left before their anticipated graduation year (Abubakar et al., 2024). These kinds of figures are more than just statistics to be mentioned in passing; they depict a university system where a sizable portion of the impacted students are already on a trajectory that is challenging, if not impossible, to reverse by the time poor academic performance appears on a final result sheet.
One of the most crucial metrics used to assess students' and training institutions' success is academic performance. Monitoring student performance is still primarily a reactive process in the majority of Nigerian universities, including the University of Port Harcourt. The chance for meaningful intervention, such as academic counselling, remedial classes, or just having a simple conversation with a struggling student, has frequently already passed for that semester by the time lecturers and academic officers learn that a student is having difficulties. This reactive stance is just what happens when an organisation lacks a methodical approach to predicting events before they happen; it is not a conscious policy decision.
Educational Data Mining (EDM) has emerged as a separate field of study over the past 20 years due to the increase in computing power and the volume of structured academic data produced by universities. Romero and Ventura (2010) documented the exponential growth of EDM publications through the 2000s in what is still one of the most widely cited reviews of the field. This growth was fuelled by the realisation that data that educational institutions had long collected—such as admissions records, continuous assessment scores, and attendance logs—could be mined for patterns that inform decision-making rather than just archived. In order to identify patterns in student data that would be challenging for a human observer to identify, such as which combination of attendance, continuous assessment (CA) performance, and prior grades is most strongly associated with a student eventually failing a course, EDM uses statistical and machine learning techniques.
Within EDM, a substantial body of work has focused specifically on the task of classification: A significant amount of work in EDM has concentrated on the task of classification: using a trained model to assign a student's record to a performance category, like pass or fail, instead of waiting for the outcome to happen naturally. Naïve Bayes, Support Vector Machines, Artificial Neural Networks, and Decision Trees are just a few of the algorithms that have been used for this task. The Decision Tree algorithm is specifically chosen for this project due to its interpretability. A Decision Tree generates a set of straightforward, traceable if-then rules that a non-technical academic staff member, like a class adviser, can read, question, and comprehend directly, in contrast to a neural network, which functions essentially as a black box even to the person who trained it. This distinction is more important in an institutional setting than it might be in a purely research setting: academic staff, not data scientists, would actually use such a system, and few institutions will act on a prediction that no one can explain. In one of the first Nigerian applications of this method, Adeyemo and Kuye (2006) showed that Decision Tree algorithms could effectively classify Nigerian students by performance level using common institutional data, and Hasan, Palaniappan, Raziff, Mahmood, and Sarker (2018) demonstrated that Decision Trees maintain competitive accuracy even when directly benchmarked against more complex models in a Middle Eastern higher education setting.
Since then, it has been more clearly shown that this approach is feasible not just in the international literature but also in a Nigerian university. Ekubo and Esiefarienrhe (2022) went beyond benchmarking to implement their chosen model as a deployed software application by applying five classification algorithms, including a Decision Tree variant, to actual undergraduate records at Niger Delta University in Bayelsa State. In order to predict dropout risk, Osemwegie and Amadin (2023), working in a computer science department at the University of Benin, applied six classifiers, including Decision Tree, to five academic sessions of actual first-year student data. This confirmed that institutional data from a Nigerian computer science department is both accessible and useful for this type of prediction task. When combined, these studies reduce the gap between what is feasible to try in a Nigerian university's Computer Science department and what the international EDM literature indicates is feasible.
The current systems in use at the Department of Computer Science, Faculty of Computing, University of Port Harcourt, such as departmental result management and portal systems, are made to record and show results after they have been calculated. They are not predictive tools; they are administrative ones. Every semester, a student's continuous assessment results, attendance, and previous grades are gathered; however, this information is not currently utilised to predict which students are at risk prior to the end of a semester. This project is specifically positioned to fill this gap.
In light of this, this project suggests designing and implementing a lightweight, web-based Students' Academic Performance Prediction System that classifies a student's likely academic performance category using a single Decision Tree algorithm based on a small, well-understood set of input variables that lecturers already have access to. In keeping with the practical requirements and time constraints of an undergraduate final year project, the system is purposefully scoped as a single-algorithm tool rather than a comparative study of multiple classifiers, giving priority to a functional, interpretable, and truly useful decision-support tool over a more comprehensive but superficial comparative exercise.
1.2 Statement of the Problem
The systems currently in use in the Department of Computer Science, as well as in many other Nigerian university departments, are made to record and display results after they have been calculated rather than to predict them beforehand. Currently, identifying which students are likely to struggle in a given semester is almost entirely dependent on the informal assessment of individual lecturers or class advisers. This approach is inconsistent from class to class, does not scale beyond a lecturer's personal attention, and is easy to fail for students who do not draw attention to themselves through obvious distress or frequent absences.
This gap has real repercussions. The cost of late detection is measured in actual academic and personal outcomes rather than just administrative inconvenience, since up to 85.05% of students who entered a Nigerian university with a third-class CGPA eventually dropped out before graduating (Abubakar et al., 2024). Under the current system, a student whose decreasing continuous assessment scores and increasing absences may have indicated a need for early intervention is indistinguishable from any other student until a final result sheet is generated, at which point the opportunity for support for the entire semester has already passed.
The department's data is not the cause of this. As part of standard administrative procedures, previous grades, attendance records, and results from ongoing assessments are already gathered each semester. It is a failure to use that data in a proactive, forward-looking manner. The department's current result management systems were not intended to forecast; rather, they were made to record and report, which is a valid and essential function. The department does not currently have a system in place that performs any analytical or predictive steps on the data it already has.
This project fills that particular gap by adding a purposefully limited, comprehensible predictive layer to the type of data that the department's current record-keeping systems and lecturers themselves already possess, rather than by replacing them. In order to predict a student's performance category early enough for that prediction to be actionable, the system designed and implemented in this project uses a single Decision Tree algorithm. It does this in a way that generates an explanation that a lecturer can examine rather than just a number that can be blindly trusted.
1.3 Aim and Objectives of the Study
Rather than being an arbitrary list of tasks, the objectives of this project are structured to follow the Knowledge Discovery in Databases (KDD) process, which is more thoroughly described in Chapter Two.
The purpose of this study is to use the Decision Tree algorithm to design and implement a web-based system for predicting students' academic performance.
The objectives of this study are to:
Review existing approaches to academic performance prediction and identify a suitable set of input variables for a Decision Tree-based model.
Design and implement a web-based system that applies a Decision Tree algorithm to classify a student's academic performance category.
Train and evaluate the Decision Tree model using standard classification metrics (accuracy, precision, and recall) with a train-test split to check for overfitting.
Assess the effectiveness of the developed system in presenting early, interpretable performance predictions to its users.
1.4 Significance of the Study
This study is important for a number of different groups, each of which is impacted by the gap it fills in a different way. Without having to decipher complex statistical results or rely on an ambiguous forecast, lecturers and academic advisers stand to gain a straightforward, comprehensible tool that identifies students who might require extra help well in advance of a semester's end. A class adviser can use the system's output as a starting point for a discussion with a student rather than just as an automated judgement to be accepted or disregarded because it can be explained in simple terms.
A functional, low-maintenance example of an applied data mining system constructed and documented to a standard that could guide future departmental projects is beneficial to the Department of Computer Science. This system's small footprint, just one trained model file and a lightweight web application makes it feasible for the department to maintain, expand, or refer to it long after this project is submitted, in contrast to systems that rely on ongoing retraining, specialised infrastructure, or outside vendor support.
Students gain materially but indirectly. Even a slight improvement in the early identification of at-risk students has a plausible connection to actual academic outcomes, not just administrative neatness, given how sharply dropout risk has been shown to rise once poor performance sets in within Nigerian university settings specifically (Abubakar et al., 2024).
Lastly, this project makes a targeted contribution to the growing corpus of literature on educational data mining in the Nigerian context. While international EDM research is extensive (Romero & Ventura, 2010), Nigerian-specific applications remain comparatively few, and fewer still progress from algorithm benchmarking to an actual deployed, documented system. This project contributes one more piece of information to the smaller but expanding body of local literature by providing an honest account of what a single interpretable Decision Tree can and cannot accomplish (Ekubo & Esiefarienrhe, 2022; Osemwegie & Amadin, 2023).
1.5 Scope of the Study
A CART Decision Tree classifier and a lightweight Flask web application serve as the foundation for this project's design and implementation of a single-algorithm, web-based academic performance prediction system. The system's scope encompasses the following: preprocessing and dataset selection; training and assessing a single Decision Tree model; creating a working web interface that allows a lecturer to submit student data and obtain an understandable prediction; and testing the final system, both in terms of the model's predictions and the behaviour of the web application.
The five academic input variables that the system uses are weekly study time, past class failures, number of absences, and first- and second-period grades. These variables were specifically chosen because a lecturer or academic adviser already regularly has access to this type of data, negating the need to develop new data collection tools or institutional procedures. In line with how the task is framed in most of the empirical literature reviewed in Chapter Two, the system assigns a student to one of three performance categories, Low, Medium, or High, rather than predicting an exact numerical score.
1.6 Limitations of the Study
There are a number of specific and intentional limitations to this study, all of which are acknowledged here rather than discovered later. First, rather than using data unique to the University of Port Harcourt, the system is trained and assessed using the publicly accessible UCI Student Performance dataset (Cortez & Silva, 2008), which was gathered from two secondary schools in Portugal. This was a conscious decision, made after it was determined that, within the timeframe of an undergraduate project, no clean, ethically approved, and publicly usable Nigerian academic dataset of comparable quality was available. This means that even though the underlying method, Decision Tree classification of academic variables, is domain-general and has been successfully applied in Nigerian studies using similar techniques, the trained model's specific numerical predictions might not generalise perfectly to University of Port Harcourt students (Adeyemo & Kuye, 2006; Ekubo & Esiefarienrhe, 2022; Osemwegie & Amadin, 2023).
Second, this study only uses and assesses one algorithm, the Decision Tree, by design. Although several of the empirical studies reviewed in Chapter Two do include comparisons and find that other algorithms occasionally outperform Decision Tree on raw accuracy, no comparative benchmarking is done against other classifiers like Naïve Bayes, Support Vector Machines, or ensemble methods like Random Forest. This study makes the narrower claim that Decision Tree is a suitable, truly interpretable option given the project's scope and intended users; it does not assert that Decision Tree is the best-performing algorithm available for this task.
Third, the system is solely web-based; no mobile application has been created, and no attempt has been made to integrate it with the department's current result management systems because doing so would introduce data-sharing and access-control considerations that are far beyond the purview of a single-algorithm, proof-of-concept academic tool.
1.7 Definition of Terms
Academic Performance: A measure of how well a student performs in their coursework, commonly represented by grades, CGPA, or a pass/fail classification.
Educational Data Mining (EDM): The application of data mining and machine learning techniques to data generated in educational settings.
Decision Tree: A supervised machine learning algorithm that classifies data by recursively splitting it into branches based on the values of its input features, forming a tree-like structure of decision rules.
Classification: A type of supervised learning task in which a model is trained to assign input data to one of a fixed set of categories.
Overfitting: A situation in which a machine learning model learns the training data too closely, including its noise, resulting in poor performance on new, unseen data.
Feature (Attribute): An individual measurable input variable used by a machine learning model, such as attendance percentage or number of past failures.
Training Set / Test Set: A split of a dataset into two parts, one used to train a model and one held back to evaluate how well it performs on unseen data.
CGPA: Cumulative Grade Point Average, a standard measure of a student's overall academic performance across all courses taken.
Dropout: The permanent withdrawal of a student from an academic programme before completing the expected duration of study.
Interpretability: The degree to which a human can understand and trace the reasoning behind a machine learning model's individual predictions.
Knowledge Discovery in Databases (KDD): A structured process for extracting useful knowledge from raw data, comprising selection, pre-processing, transformation, data mining, and interpretation/evaluation.
Continuous Assessment (CA): Coursework-based scores, such as tests, assignments, and quizzes, accumulated over a semester and used alongside examination scores to determine a student's final grade.
CHAPTER TWOLITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Overview
According to Chapter One, there is currently no predictive step between the data that Nigerian universities routinely gather and the academic outcomes that the data could predict. This includes the Department of Computer Science at the University of Port Harcourt. This chapter lays the groundwork for bridging that gap in the literature. It starts with a conceptual framework of the main concepts involved, then goes into detail about the Decision Tree algorithm and the theoretical process that guided this study, followed by a broad empirical review of what the literature generally finds and a focused examination of the state and future direction of Educational Data Mining research in Nigeria specifically; and, finally, a detailed review of the individual studies that are most directly related to the design decisions made for this project.
2.2 Conceptual Framework
Academic performance, educational data mining, and classification are the three main ideas of this study. Before any of the more technical information that follows makes sense, it is important to understand how they relate to each other.
In this study, "academic performance" refers to a student's quantifiable success in their coursework, which is usually represented by grades, Cumulative Grade Point Average (CGPA), and continuous assessment (CA) scores. It is influenced by a combination of non-academic elements like family history, health, and socioeconomic status, as well as academic elements like attendance, previous grades, and study time. Particularly in Nigerian universities, it has been demonstrated that factors like gender, sponsorship type, mode of admission, and course of study have a statistically significant impact on whether a student eventually finishes their degree or drops out (Abubakar et al., 2024). Therefore, academic performance is not a single static number but rather the result of a combination of measurable variables, which is exactly the type of structure that a classification algorithm is best suited to model.
The application of data mining techniques, typically utilised in commercial domains like retail and finance, to data generated within educational systems is known as educational data mining, or EDM. It uses the more comprehensive Knowledge Discovery in Databases (KDD) process, which is covered in detail in section 2.4, to identify hitherto unidentified, potentially helpful patterns in unprocessed academic data. Because universities now routinely produce large volumes of structured student data, such as admission records, continuous assessment scores, and attendance logs, that were previously too large or simply too unexamined to analyse by hand, EDM has developed into a distinct research field.
The particular EDM task that this study focuses on is classification, which is a supervised learning task where a model is trained on a labelled dataset, that is, a dataset where each example's correct outcome is already known so that it can learn to assign new, unseen examples to the appropriate category. The trained model's task in this project is to predict the performance category, Low, Medium, or High, of a new student record that it has never seen before. The labelled examples are historical student records with known performance outcomes.
Together, these three ideas make up the conceptual framework of this study: classification is the particular method used to link the two, academic performance is the outcome being modelled, and EDM is the larger methodological field this project falls under. Since the technical architecture diagram itself is presented in Chapter Three, Figure 2.1, which is conceptually presented here in prose, can be understood as three nested layers: EDM as a field at the widest layer, classification as the specific task within it, and the Decision Tree as the particular algorithm selected to carry it out within that. This chapter's subsequent sections delve one level further into this structure. Starting with the algorithm selected to carry out the classification task, the rest of this chapter looks at each layer of that framework in increasingly more technical and empirical detail.
2.3 The Decision Tree Algorithm
One of the most basic and popular classification algorithms is a decision tree. Using the value of an input feature, it recursively divides a dataset into smaller subsets, selecting at each stage the feature that best divides the data in relation to the target class.
2.3.1 How a Decision Tree Works
The entire training dataset is stored at the root node where a decision tree is first constructed. The algorithm assesses each feature at each node and chooses the one that, if the dataset were divided on it, would result in the biggest improvement in class separation. Until each subset, known as a leaf node, is reasonably pure, that is, it contains primarily instances of a single class, or until a stopping condition, such as a maximum tree depth or a minimum number of samples per leaf, is met, this process recursively repeats at each resulting child node, creating branches (Quinlan, 1986; Breiman, Friedman, Olshen, & Stone, 1984). After training, a new, unseen record is classified by beginning at the root and following the branch at each node that corresponds to its feature values until it reaches a leaf node and, consequently, a predicted class.
2.3.2 Splitting Criteria: Gini Index and Information Gain
To determine which feature yields the best split at a particular node, two metrics are typically employed. The ID3 and C4.5 algorithms use information gain, which is based on the idea of entropy from information theory and quantifies the decrease in uncertainty about the class label attained by a particular split (Quinlan, 1986). A Gini index of 0 denotes perfect purity. The Gini index quantifies the frequency with which a randomly selected record would be incorrectly classified if it were labelled in accordance with the distribution of classes in a subset. The scikit-learn library for Python is used in this project to implement the Classification and Regression Trees (CART) formulation, which by default employs the Gini index (Breiman et al., 1984; Han, Kamber, & Pei, 2011).
2.3.3 Decision Tree Variants (ID3, C4.5, and CART)
The Decision Tree algorithm has multiple implementations, and they are not interchangeable. One of the first formulations was ID3 (Iterative Dichotomizer 3), which used information gain and performed best with categorical attributes. By adding pruning to lessen overfitting and supporting continuous attributes, C4.5 expanded on ID3. In contrast to the multi-way splits generated by ID3 or C4.5, the formulation used in this project, CART, supports both classification and regression, uses the Gini index by default, and consistently produces binary splits, making its resulting trees somewhat easier to visualise and interpret (Breiman et al., 1984). This project is explicit about using CART rather than treating "Decision Tree" as a single, uniform technique because Hamoud, Hashim, and Awadh (2018) empirically showed that different Decision Tree variants, specifically J48 (a Java implementation of C4.5), Random Tree, and REPTree, can produce meaningfully different accuracy on the same underlying student dataset.
2.3.4 Advantages and Disadvantages of the Decision Tree Algorithm
The main benefit of the Decision Tree algorithm, which is why it was chosen for this project, is its interpretability: the final model is a collection of nested if-then rules that a non-technical user, like a lecturer or class adviser, can read, trace, and explain. Additionally, Decision Trees handle both numerical and categorical features naturally, require relatively little data preprocessing, and are computationally cheap to train on datasets the size of an undergraduate project. By limiting the maximum tree depth and assessing performance on a held-out test set, this project mitigates their main drawback, which is their propensity to overfit the training data if grown without restriction, resulting in a tree that memorises noise in the training set rather than learning generalisable patterns. Another drawback is that a single Decision Tree is frequently marginally outperformed in raw accuracy by ensemble techniques like Random Forest or by less comprehensible models like Artificial Neural Networks, as shown by the empirical literature reviewed in section 2.8. This project consciously accepts that trade-off, giving interpretability precedence over slight increases in accuracy.
2.3.5 A Worked Illustration of a Single Split
A concrete illustration makes the abstract description above easier to understand. Assume that out of 100 students in a training set, 60 eventually achieved a Medium or High performance category, while 40 eventually achieved a Low performance category. The 100 students are divided into two groups if the algorithm takes into account splitting on second-period grade (G2) at a threshold of 9.5: those with G2 at or below 9.5 and those above it. In the event that the first group has 45 Low-performing students and only 5 Medium/High students, and the second group has 35 Medium/High students and only 15 Low students, this split has resulted in two subsets that are significantly more pure and dominated by a single class than the initial mixed set of 100. The algorithm evaluates this candidate split against all other features and thresholds it could have selected, choosing the one that yields the biggest improvement. The Gini index quantifies precisely this improvement numerically. After that, this procedure is repeated independently inside each of the two resulting subsets until the previously mentioned stopping conditions are met. The actual split thresholds and class counts obtained for this project are reported in Chapter Four; the worked figures used here are illustrative rather than derived from the actual trained model.
2.4 Theoretical Framework
The Knowledge Discovery in Databases (KDD) process model put forth by Fayyad, Piatetsky-Shapiro, and Smyth (1996) serves as the foundation for this investigation. Each of the five general stages that the KDD process outlines for transforming raw data into knowledge that can be put to use corresponds directly to a step in the methodology used in this project's third chapter.
A few studies reviewed in section 2.8, such as Ekubo and Esiefarienrhe (2022), adopt CRISP-DM (Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining), a six-phase framework commonly used in industry data mining projects. It is worthwhile to briefly defend this decision against an alternative. The practical distinction between CRISP-DM and KDD is that the former adds an explicit "business understanding" phase focused on commercial deployment contexts, while the latter adds an explicit "deployment" phase as a separate final step. For this particular project, KDD was chosen because, in contrast to CRISP-DM's more industry- and stakeholder-orientated framing, its five stages map more directly onto an academic, single-developer project. This eliminates the need to artificially translate concepts like "business understanding" into an academic setting where there is no actual business.
2.4.1 Selection
Finding and collecting the information pertinent to the current task is the first step. In the context of this project, this means acquiring the UCI Student Performance dataset (Cortez & Silva, 2008) and determining which of its characteristics are pertinent to performance category prediction.
2.4.2 Pre-processing
Cleaning the chosen data is the second step, which includes addressing missing values, fixing inconsistencies, and eliminating unnecessary or redundant records. This relates to the process of cleaning the data before using it for training that was done in Chapter 3.
2.4.3 Transformation
In the third step, the cleaned data is transformed into a format that the mining algorithm can use. For example, categorical variables can be numerically encoded, or a discrete performance category can be derived from a continuous grade. This aligns with the feature engineering phase of the project's methodology.
2.4.4 Data Mining
In order to identify patterns and create a predictive model, the fourth step involves applying the selected algorithm, in this case, the CART Decision Tree to the converted data.
2.4.5 Interpretation and Evaluation
In the last step, the extracted patterns are evaluated for utility and presented in a way that the intended user can act upon. In this project, this means that the trained model is assessed using accuracy, precision, and recall, and its predictions are displayed via a straightforward web interface designed for academic advisers and lecturers. The KDD model does not go beyond the goals of this project, in contrast to a comprehensive learning theory or pedagogical framework. This is in line with the minimalist design principle that underpins the entire system.
2.5 Empirical Review
It is helpful to take a step back and consider what the broader empirical literature generally finds about machine learning-based academic performance prediction before delving into specific studies in section 2.8. This is because patterns that recur across numerous independent studies are more significant than any single finding.
Rather than clustering around a single expected figure, the most consistent finding across this literature is that reported accuracy varies significantly depending on the number of output classes, the size of the dataset, and the features used. Using a modestly sized Bulgarian university dataset, Kabakchieva (2013) reported prediction accuracy ranging from 52% to 67% across five performance classes. In contrast, accuracies in the high 90s were reported by Ekubo and Esiefarienrhe (2022) and Osemwegie and Amadin (2023), who both used larger, actual Nigerian institutional datasets with fewer output classes. Studies employing coarser, binary, or three-class targets, as this project does, should reasonably expect higher accuracy than studies attempting finer-grained multi-class prediction. This is not a contradiction between studies; rather, it reflects a broader empirical pattern that classification difficulty rises sharply with the number of categories being distinguished.
A second recurring pattern relates to how well Decision Tree performs in comparison to other algorithms. A single Decision Tree is rarely the best algorithm in a head-to-head comparison across the studies reviewed in this chapter: Random Forest defeated it in Hasan, Palaniappan, Raziff, Mahmood, and Sarker (2018); and Multilayer Perceptron and Logistic Regression defeated it in Ekubo and Esiefarienrhe (2022) and Osemwegie and Amadin (2023), respectively. However, Decision Tree's accuracy did not lag significantly in any of these studies; rather, it remained competitive with the best algorithm, usually within a few percentage points. This is the empirical foundation for the main design choice of this project: the literature supports treating Decision Tree as a strong, defensible option on their own merits, as long as the objective is a practical, comprehensible system rather than the highest possible accuracy score.
Which input features have the highest predictive weight is the subject of a third pattern. Prior academic performance, whether expressed as prior grades, GPA, or earlier assessment scores, consistently emerges as the strongest predictor across studies that report feature importance or comparable measures, significantly outperforming demographic or social variables (Cortez & Silva, 2008; Baradwaj & Pal, 2011; Kabra & Bichkar, 2011). This directly and practically affects the feature selection for this project, which is covered in more detail in Chapter Three: a small set of academic variables is not just a convenient simplification but, based on the larger body of literature, is close to what actually drives predictive accuracy in this task.
Lastly, a pattern that is noteworthy due to its relative lack is that relatively few of the studies examined in this chapter go beyond simply reporting classification results in a tool like WEKA to actually creating and implementing a functional software system based on their selected model. This observation is expanded upon as the literature gap for this chapter in section 2.11, but it is important to highlight it here as an independent empirical pattern rather than just a gap that this project has discovered.
Additionally, it is noteworthy that the studies reviewed in this chapter are not entirely consistent in which evaluation metric they treat as primary. This is a methodological observation rather than an algorithmic finding. While some studies, like Kabakchieva (2013), use accuracy as the primary figure, others, like Osemwegie and Amadin (2023), base their final model on F1-score and recall specifically because they believe that accuracy alone can be deceptive when classes are unbalanced, which is common in academic performance data where a Medium category usually contains far more students than a High or Low category. This project takes a more cautious approach: Chapter Three specifies accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score collectively rather than accuracy alone. This is because a model that appears strong on accuracy alone may still be performing poorly on a minority class; this distinction is important for a tool designed to specifically flag at-risk students.
2.6 Current State of Educational Data Mining Research in Nigeria
Romero and Ventura (2010) documented several hundred EDM publications and an exponential growth trajectory through the 2000s alone in one of the field's most widely cited reviews. Educational Data Mining has grown into a significant international research field. In contrast, the amount of EDM research that is specifically focused on Nigeria is still much smaller, even though it is not new. Decision tree-based academic performance mining was first used in Nigeria by Adeyemo and Kuye (2006), long before EDM was a recognised field of computer science research in Nigeria.
More recent research from Nigeria indicates that the field is developing rather than just enduring. Osemwegie and Amadin (2023) applied six classifiers to five academic sessions of real first-year Computer Science student data at the University of Benin specifically to predict dropout risk, while Ekubo and Esiefarienrhe (2022) went beyond algorithm benchmarking to implement a deployed software application using real institutional data from Niger Delta University. Both studies (2022 and 2023) were carried out in computer science departments that resemble the one this project is located in, and they both use actual Nigerian institutional data rather than artificial or foreign datasets.
However, compared to the scope of the international literature Romero and Ventura (2010) describe, the overall volume of Nigerian-specific EDM research is still small, and studies that combine real Nigerian data with a deployed, interpretable, and user-facing system as opposed to either historical benchmarking alone or an interface without a rigorously evaluated model behind it remain even more rare. This project places itself within this particular gap in the current Nigerian EDM landscape, which is covered more formally in section 2.11 of this chapter as the literature gap.
Practical rather than technical factors have contributed to the slower growth of EDM research in Nigeria compared to the global literature. Access to actual, ethically approved, institution-level student data is still a real limitation, which this project directly faced and resolved by using a public international dataset instead of an unverified local one, as described in Chapter One. Studies like Ekubo and Esiefarienrhe (2022) and Osemwegie and Amadin (2023), which did obtain access to actual institutional data, stand out as relatively rare rather than typical examples in the local literature because of this issue, which is not specific to Nigeria but is felt more keenly where formal data-sharing and research-ethics infrastructure around student records are still developing.
2.7 Opportunities and Future Directions for EDM in Nigerian Higher Education
The current situation outlined in section 2.6 directly leads to a number of opportunities. First, Nigerian computer science departments already produce precisely the kind of organised, regularly gathered data such as continuous assessment scores, attendance, and previous grades that EDM research relies on. The lack of readily available, ethically approved, publicly usable datasets and the lack of implemented tools that transform departmental data into an actionable decision-support resource rather than an archival record are the main obstacles.
Second, Romero and Ventura (2010) clearly identify interpretability and the practical adoption of EDM tools by non-specialist educational staff as an under-served direction for the field generally. This is not a Nigeria-specific observation, but it applies particularly here: the Nigerian studies reviewed in this chapter that did make it to deployment (Ekubo & Esiefarienrhe, 2022; Osemwegie & Amadin, 2023) prioritised predictive accuracy in their algorithm selection, which is exactly the direction.
Third, at the undergraduate final-year project level, where the scope, timeframe, and single-developer constraints of a project like this one make a lean, single-algorithm, genuinely deployed system a more achievable target than a broad multi-algorithm comparative study, there is a realistic opportunity for future Nigerian EDM research to close the gap between algorithm benchmarking and actual institutional deployment identified in section 2.6. When real, ethically sourced University of Port Harcourt student data becomes available, future work building on this project could logically expand it. It could also assess whether the interpretability benefits shown here significantly alter how lecturers actually use the system's predictions in practice a question that this project's own scope, outlined in Chapter One, does not attempt to answer.
The project's design also leads to more focused technical opportunities. Since the underlying model and interface do not assume any specific course or dataset beyond the five input features already generalised, the single-table, single-algorithm architecture described in Chapter Three would naturally extend to a departmental pilot covering more than one course or cohort without requiring a fundamental redesign. Similarly, even though this project purposefully avoids ensemble methods for the reasons covered in section 2.5, a logical next step for future work would be to compare this project's depth-limited Decision Tree against a similarly constrained Random Forest, specifically to quantify, on the same Nigerian departmental data, how much interpretability is actually being traded away for how much additional accuracy. This is a question that the international literature reviewed in this chapter only addresses in aggregate, not for any particular Nigerian institution.
2.8 Review of Related Literature
This section, which is arranged into four thematic groups rather than a single undifferentiated list, thoroughly examines the particular empirical studies that are most pertinent to the algorithm, dataset type, and application domain chosen for this project.
2.8.1 Foundational Decision-Tree-Based Prediction Studies
In order to identify academically weak students at the higher secondary level in India, Ramaswami and Bhaskaran (2010) developed a CHAID-based (Chi-square Automatic Interaction Detector) predictive model. Using a seven-class response variable and a dataset of 772 student records gathered from five schools in three districts of Tamil Nadu, the study found that the best indicators of performance were the type of secondary education, school location, prior exam scores, and the medium of instruction. The study is an early and significant demonstration that tree-based methods can extract interpretable, actionable rules from actual academic data, which is the same underlying premise this project is based on, even though CHAID is a Decision Tree variant different from the CART formulation used in this project.
Baradwaj and Pal (2011) used variables like prior semester grades, class test scores, seminar performance, and attendance to analyse undergraduate students' performance using a Decision Tree-based classification approach. Their model was able to identify students who were likely to perform poorly early on and classify students into performance categories. This project also relies on the study's early demonstration that easily accessible institutional data, without any specialised data collection effort, is sufficient to build a usable predictive classifier, which is often cited in subsequent Nigerian and international EDM research.
In order to identify students at risk of failing the final exam, Kabra and Bichkar (2011) employed a Decision Tree algorithm to predict engineering students' performance based on their internal assessment scores and past academic record. Their findings supported the project's choice to rely on a small, minimal feature set, continuous assessment scores, attendance, and previous grades rather than an exhaustive list of student attributes by demonstrating that a relatively small number of carefully selected internal assessment variables could produce meaningfully accurate early predictions.
At a time when machine learning was still not widely used in Nigerian academic research, Adeyemo and Kuye (2006) carried out one of the first Nigerian studies using decision tree algorithms to mine students' academic performance data. Their work established an early local precedent for the methodology used in this project by demonstrating that Decision Tree techniques could effectively classify Nigerian students by performance level using institutional data.
2.8.2 Comparative Algorithm Studies
At a Bulgarian university, Kabakchieva (2013) conducted a data mining study that was organised around the four stages of business understanding, data understanding, data preprocessing, and modelling. The study compared Decision Tree, Bayesian classifiers, and k-Nearest Neighbour algorithms for predicting student performance. Across the five performance classes used in the study, reported prediction accuracies ranged from 52% to 67%; these numbers are significantly lower than some of the accuracies reported elsewhere in this review. This study serves as a helpful counterweight in the literature, as discussed in section 2.5. It shows that Decision Tree-based prediction does not always yield high accuracy, especially when there are more output classes, and it supports the project's choice to set reasonable expectations for model accuracy rather than assuming near-perfect results are typical.
Using a small dataset of 22 undergraduate students from a private higher education institution in Oman, Hasan, Palaniappan, Raziff, Mahmood, and Sarker (2018) investigated the effectiveness of Decision Tree algorithms using academic data and Learning Management System (Moodle) activity data gathered for a core Electronic Commerce Technologies module. Their findings, which were obtained using the WEKA data mining tool, demonstrated that although a standard Decision Tree generated predictions that could be used, a Random Forest variant performed marginally better in terms of accuracy. This result exemplifies the recurrent trade-off between the interpretability and simplicity of a single Decision Tree and the slightly higher accuracy of ensemble methods, which is deliberately accepted in favour of interpretability in this project's design. This trade-off is covered in more detail in section 2.5.
Using survey-based student data, Hamoud, Hashim, and Awadh (2018) compared three Decision Tree variants J48, Random Tree, and REPTree, with the specific goal of determining which Decision Tree implementation worked best for predicting academic performance. Rather than treating "Decision Tree" as a single, undifferentiated technique, this project's decision to be explicit in Chapter Three about which implementation, CART, via Python's scikit-learn library, is being used is directly supported by their finding that different Decision Tree implementations produced meaningfully different levels of accuracy on the same underlying data.
2.8.3 Nigerian Departmental Deployments
Using actual undergraduate records from Niger Delta University in Bayelsa State, Nigeria, Ekubo and Esiefarienrhe (2022) used the CRISP-DM methodology and the WEKA tool to compare five classification algorithms: J48 Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, Multilayer Perceptron, Naïve Bayes and Sequential Minimal Optimisation. The Decision Tree-based classifier was competitive among the five, even though a Multilayer Perceptron ultimately yielded the highest accuracy. Notably, the authors achieved 98% accuracy on their test data by implementing their chosen model as a deployed PHP/Python software application, going beyond benchmarking. This is the Nigerian literature's most structurally similar precedent to the current project, proving that a complete pipeline from data mining research to a functional, deployed academic software tool can be accomplished within a similar scope.
In order to predict student dropout, Osemwegie and Amadin (2023) used five academic sessions of actual first-year student data (906 usable records after cleaning) from the Department of Computer Science at the University of Benin, Nigeria. After training and comparison, six classifiers, Naïve Bayes, Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, Decision Tree, k-Nearest Neighbour, and Artificial Neural Network, achieved average performance of 90.4%, 98.9%, 98.5%, 97.4%, 96.0%, and 97.3%, respectively. The Decision Tree classifier performed competitively at 97.4%, even though Logistic Regression was ultimately chosen for deployment based on F1 score and recall. One of the most directly applicable studies to the current project is this one, which verifies that institutional student data from a Nigerian computer science department can be used for this type of prediction task and that Decision Tree is still a reliable, powerful option even when compared to more sophisticated classifiers.
2.8.4 The Source Dataset Study
The dataset used for training and evaluation in this project was gathered and published by Cortez and Silva (2008). It consists of actual academic records of secondary school students from two Portuguese schools, covering the subjects of mathematics and Portuguese, as well as a variety of demographic, social, and school-related characteristics like study time, the number of prior class failures, family educational support, and alcohol consumption. Their initial study used a number of data mining techniques, such as Decision Tree, to forecast final-period grades. They discovered that, predictably, prior-period grades (G1 and G2) were the best indicators of final performance (G3), while social and demographic characteristics alone contributed relatively little predictive value. In addition to being a part of the larger empirical literature on Decision Tree-based performance prediction, this study serves as a direct methodological reference for the feature selection and evaluation approach because this dataset was used in Chapter Three of this project.
2.8.5 Cross-Cutting Observations Across the Reviewed Studies
When the fourteen studies mentioned above are viewed as a single body of work rather than as discrete findings, three observations come to light that individually influenced design choices made throughout this project. First, sample sizes vary greatly, ranging from 22 students (Hasan et al., 2018) to 906 (Osemwegie & Amadin, 2023) to 772 (Ramaswami & Bhaskaran, 2010). Additionally, reported accuracy does not simply correlate with sample size; the largest study did not report the highest accuracy, and the smallest did not report the lowest. This lessens the temptation to treat the 395 records in this project's dataset as intrinsically too small to yield a meaningful result, since studies of similar or smaller size elsewhere in this review produced useful, publishable results.
Second, while the institutional and geographic contexts vary greatly, particularly in India, Bulgaria, Oman, Iraq, and Nigeria, the fundamental empirical pattern of prior academic performance dominating feature importance and Decision Tree remaining competitive but rarely best holds fairly consistently across all of them. The idea that a non-Nigerian dataset can still produce a methodologically sound and broadly applicable model even though its specific numerical predictions may not transfer perfectly to University of Port Harcourt students is somewhat supported by this, as it implies that the underlying relationship this project models is not a narrowly local artefact of any one educational system.
Third, none of the fourteen studies that were examined included all three of the following in a single project: an actual deployed, tested, functional software system; a single carefully selected algorithm that is primarily defended on interpretability rather than accuracy; and real or established data. Only one or two of these three are attained by individual studies; none accomplish all three at once. This project occupies this exact, specific space, which is formally restated as the literature gap in section 2.11.
2.9 Review of Enabling Technologies
In keeping with the purposefully minimal technology stack outlined in Chapter One, this section examines the particular software tools and technologies used for the system's design and implementation. In keeping with the project's overarching design principle of avoiding needless complexity, each tool was selected for a single, clearly defined role.
2.9.1 Python
Because of its readable syntax and extensive ecosystem of scientific computing libraries, Python is a high-level, general-purpose programming language that is extensively used in data science and machine learning. Because it can be used to clean and prepare the dataset, train and assess the Decision Tree model, and serve the resulting predictions through a web application, it was selected as the primary language for this project. This eliminates the additional complexity of coordinating multiple languages across the data pipeline and the web layer.
2.9.2 Flask
Flask is a lightweight WSGI web framework for Python that is frequently referred to as a "microframework" because it only offers the fundamental elements required to create a web application, such as routing, request handling, and templating, leaving other choices, like database access or authentication, up to the developer (Grinberg, 2018). Because the web interface for this project is compact and serves only one purpose, one form for input, one page for results, and an optional history table. Flask was selected over a more complex framework like Django. While a full-featured framework would add structure and configuration that the project does not require, a microframework directly matches this scope.
2.9.3 Scikit-learn
Built on top of NumPy and SciPy, Scikit-learn is an open-source Python machine learning library that offers effective, tested implementations of popular machine learning algorithms, such as the CART formulation of the Decision Tree algorithm used in this project (Pedregosa et al., 2011). It was selected because it is the de facto standard tool for classical machine learning tasks in Python, has extensive documentation, and has built-in tools for dividing data into training and test sets, calculating accuracy, precision, and recall, and visualising a trained Decision Tree all of which are directly utilised in the methodology and evaluation of this project.
2.9.4 SQLite
Without the need to install, configure, or maintain a separate database server process, SQLite is a lightweight, serverless, file-based relational database engine that stores an entire database as a single file on disc. It was selected over a client-server database like MySQL or PostgreSQL due to the system's minimal data storage requirements, the optional table of historical predictions, and the lack of meaningful concurrent multi-user write access. The minimalism principle that guides the rest of the system's design would be violated by using a heavier database engine, which would increase operational overhead without a corresponding benefit in the scope of this project.
2.10 Summary of Reviewed Literature
The conceptual relationship between academic performance, EDM, and classification; the mechanics, variations, and trade-offs of the Decision Tree algorithm in particular; the KDD process as the theoretical framework for this study; a broad empirical synthesis demonstrating that Decision Tree consistently performs competitively, though rarely best, across widely varying accuracy ranges and dataset sizes; the current, still modest but maturing state of Nigerian EDM research; realistic opportunities for future work in this space; and fourteen individual studies reviewed in detail across four thematic groups. When taken as a whole, the literature supports two conclusions that form the basis of this project's design: first, that a single, interpretable Decision Tree is a reasonable, evidence-based decision rather than a compromise; second, that a deployed, functional system based on that decision is still relatively uncommon in the Nigerian literature, which is where this project's own contribution is situated.
2.11 Literature Gap
Two recurrent themes emerge from a close examination of the literature reviewed in this chapter. First, the vast majority of these studies, both Nigerian and foreign, end at the benchmarking stage. Algorithms are trained and compared, usually in WEKA, and results are reported; however, a functional, deployed software system is rarely developed as part of the same study. A notable Nigerian exception is Ekubo and Esiefarienrhe (2022), while Osemwegie and Amadin (2023) also refrained from full deployment even though they used actual departmental data. Second, very few of the reviewed studies treat interpretability as the primary design goal; instead, algorithm choice is typically determined by raw accuracy, with Decision Tree being included primarily as a baseline to be outperformed by more complex methods rather than as the intentional, final choice.
Despite its limited scope, this project's contribution directly addresses both patterns: it goes beyond benchmarking to create a real, usable, web-based decision-support tool, and it purposefully chooses a single algorithm based on interpretability rather than trying to achieve state-of-the-art accuracy through comparison or ensembling. This is in line with the realistic limitations of an undergraduate project and, one could argue, with what an actual academic department would be willing to accept and rely on on a daily basis.

CHAPTER THREESYSTEM ANALYSIS AND METHODOLOGY
3.1 Methodology of the Study
In keeping with its two components, this study employs two complementary methodologies. The Knowledge Discovery in Databases (KDD) process, which was previously presented as the theoretical framework for this study in Chapter Two, is followed by the data mining component (dataset selection, preprocessing, model training, and evaluation). A single developer, as opposed to a team, completes the requirements definition, design, implementation, and testing steps of the straightforward, linear build sequence for the software component (the web application that serves the trained model).
This purposefully does not adhere to a team-oriented methodology like Rapid Application Development or Agile/Scrum, both of which are present in a number of the department's other project reports. These approaches, which are based on sprint cycles, cross-functional cooperation, and ongoing stakeholder engagement, don't really apply to an undergraduate project with just one developer and one algorithm. The way the system was actually constructed would be misrepresented if that language were used here. Instead, a linear sequence that is in line with the minimalism principle outlined in Chapter One and proportionate to the project's scope was chosen.
In practical terms, the build went as follows: (i) the UCI Student Performance dataset was acquired and its characteristics were compared to the input variables found in Chapter Two's literature review; (ii) the current manual method for identifying at-risk students was examined to determine what the suggested system needed to improve on; (iii) the Decision Tree model was trained and assessed offline; (iv) the Flask web application was constructed around the trained model; and (v) the entire system was tested, both at the level of the model's predictions and the web application's routes.
3.2 Justification of Methodology Used
Rather than being taken for granted, the combined KDD-and-linear approach used in this study is supported on the following particular grounds:
Proportionality to project scope: Since there is no cross-functional team to coordinate, no stakeholder group to conduct sprint reviews with, and no competing feature backlog to prioritise, the basic reasoning behind Agile's ceremonies simply does not apply when a single developer is creating a single-algorithm system.
Direct alignment with the technical task: unlike general-purpose software methodologies that are not built around a data mining pipeline, the five stages of the KDD process (selection, pre-processing, transformation, data mining, interpretation/evaluation) map one-to-one onto the actual sequence of scripts and decisions used to build this system, which is fully described in section 3.1.
Reproducibility: Iterative approaches are more difficult to audit after the fact because they are non-linear by design, whereas a supervisor or examiner can easily follow a linear, clearly ordered sequence and verify step-by-step against the actual codebase.
Sincerity regarding scale: claiming an enterprise-oriented methodology for a project this size would exaggerate rather than accurately depict its complexity, which is in direct opposition to the minimalism principle upheld throughout this study.
Precedent in the reviewed literature: For a similarly scoped academic prediction system, Ekubo and Esiefarienrhe (2022), the most structurally similar prior work found in Chapter Two, employed CRISP-DM, a closely related data-mining-first methodology, instead of a general software engineering methodology.

3.6 Functional and Non-Functional Requirements
3.6.1 Functional Requirements
The system shall allow a user to submit a student's study time, past failures, absences, and first and second period grades through a web form.
The system shall classify the submitted record into one of three performance categories: Low, Medium, or High, using a trained Decision Tree model.
The system shall display the predicted category alongside the model's confidence for each possible category.
The system shall identify and display the single most influential factor behind the model's predictions.
The system shall log each prediction, with its inputs and result, to a persistent local database.
The system shall provide a page summarising the trained model's evaluation metrics (accuracy, precision, recall) for transparency.
3.6.2 Non-Functional Requirements
Usability: the interface shall require no technical or statistical background to operate or interpret.
Performance: a prediction shall be returned to the user in under one second, since the model is loaded once at application startup rather than retrained per request.
Portability: the system shall run on a standard developer machine without requiring a dedicated database server or cloud infrastructure.
Maintainability: the trained model shall be swappable by replacing a single serialized file (decision_tree_model.joblib) without changes to the application code.
3.7 Dataset and Feature Selection
The system is trained on the UCI Student Performance dataset (student-mat.csv), collected by Cortez and Silva (2008) from two Portuguese secondary schools and reviewed in detail in Chapter Two. The raw dataset contains 395 student records across 33 attributes, covering demographic, social, and academic variables.
Consistent with the minimalism principle established in Chapter One, only five attributes are used as model inputs, deliberately excluding demographic and social attributes (such as family size, parental education, or romantic relationship status) that Cortez and Silva (2008) themselves found contributed comparatively little predictive value on their own:
studytime — weekly study time, on a 1 to 4 scale.
failures — number of past class failures.
absences — number of school absences.
G1 — first period grade (0 to 20).
G2 — second period grade (0 to 20).
The target variable is derived, not taken directly from the dataset. The dataset's continuous final grade, G3 (0 to 20), is converted into a three-class categorical target using thresholds consistent with the dataset's own documentation of a 10-mark pass threshold: Low (G3 below 10), Medium (G3 from 10 to 14), and High (G3 of 15 or above). This mirrors the classification framing used in the majority of the empirical studies reviewed in Chapter Two, rather than treating this as a regression problem.
3.8 Model Development Approach
The dataset was split into training and test subsets using an 80/20 split (316 training records, 79 test records), stratified by performance category to preserve the same class proportions in both subsets. Stratification matters here because the dataset's classes are not evenly distributed (Medium is the most common outcome, High the least common); an unstratified split risks a test set that misrepresents the true class balance.
The model itself is a CART (Classification and Regression Trees) Decision Tree, implemented via scikit-learn's DecisionTreeClassifier, using the Gini index as its splitting criterion. Two parameters were set deliberately to control overfitting, consistent with the risk identified in Chapter Two: maximum tree depth was capped at 4, and a minimum of 5 samples was required per leaf node. Both constraints were chosen to keep the resulting tree small enough to remain genuinely interpretable, in line with this project's central design argument, rather than tuned purely to maximise test accuracy.
Model evaluation uses accuracy, weighted precision, weighted recall, and weighted F1-score, together with a full confusion matrix, rather than accuracy alone. Weighted averaging was used, rather than macro averaging, because it accounts for the dataset's class imbalance rather than treating the rare High-performance class as equally weighted to the far more common Medium class. The actual results of this evaluation, run against the real trained model, are presented in Chapter Four.

4.4.2 Walkthrough of the Request Flow
When a lecturer submits the prediction form, the Flask /predict route (POST) validates the five numeric fields; if any are missing or invalid, the form is re-rendered with an inline error rather than failing silently. On valid input, the five values are assembled into a feature vector matching the order used at training time, passed to the loaded DecisionTreeClassifier's predict() and predict_proba() methods, and the feature with the highest global importance is looked up from the saved evaluation results to present as the 'top contributing factor'. The submission, its inputs, and the resulting prediction are then written to the predictions table before the result page is rendered. 4.7 Software Maintenance
Three maintenance scenarios are anticipated for this system, each handled without requiring changes to the core application code in app.py.
Retraining the model on updated data: replacing student-mat.csv with a new dataset of the same column structure and rerunning train_model.py regenerates decision_tree_model.joblib and model_results.json; the Flask application picks up the new model automatically on its next restart, since it always loads whichever file is currently on disk.
Adjusting the interpretability/accuracy trade-off: the max_depth and min_samples_leaf parameters in train_model.py, discussed in Chapter Three, section 3.8, are the two values to change if a future maintainer wants a deeper, potentially more accurate tree at the cost of interpretability, or a shallower one for the reverse trade-off.
Clearing prediction history: deleting predictions.db and restarting the application recreates an empty predictions table automatically, since the schema is defined in code (app.py's init_db function) rather than requiring a separate migration step.
4.8 Discussion of Results
Returning to the objectives set out in Chapter One: Objective 3 (train and evaluate the model, checking for overfitting via a train/test split) is satisfied, with the model achieving 84.81% accuracy on genuinely unseen test data. Objective 4 (assess the system's effectiveness in presenting early, interpretable predictions) is supported by the interface shown in section 4.4, which surfaces not just a predicted category but a stated top contributing factor and per-class confidence for every prediction.
The dominance of G2 in Figure 4.3 (96.4% of total feature importance) deserves direct comment rather than being glossed over. It means this particular trained model is, practically speaking, close to a threshold rule on second-period grade, with the other four features acting as minor adjustments rather than equal contributors. This is not a flaw introduced by the implementation; it independently reproduces the finding already reported by Cortez and Silva (2008) in the dataset's own original study and echoed by Kabra and Bichkar (2011) that prior-period performance is by far the strongest predictor of final academic outcomes. It does, however, meaningfully qualify the system's practical value: a lecturer who already has a student's G2 could reasonably approximate this model's output without running it. The system's genuine value lies less in G2 alone and more in formalising that judgement consistently, attaching a confidence level to it, and folding in the smaller but real contributions of absences, G1, and study time, rather than in discovering a hidden pattern invisible to an experienced lecturer.
The confusion matrix (Figure 4.1) shows the model's errors are concentrated at the Low/Medium boundary rather than spread randomly, which is the behaviour expected of a threshold-sensitive classifier operating near a class boundary and is consistent with the modest-accuracy findings reported by Kabakchieva (2013) for multi-class academic performance prediction in Chapter Two's literature review, rather than an implementation defect.
Taken together, these results support the central argument made in Chapter Two: a single, depth-limited Decision Tree does not achieve the highest possible accuracy that a more complex model or an ensemble might reach, but it produces a fully traceable, auditable prediction, visible in Figure 4.2, that a lecturer can inspect and question directly. That trade-off, made deliberately in Chapter One and defended throughout Chapter Two, is what this chapter's results confirm was actually delivered.
